# How representative are the two case-study corridors?

Added 2026-10-22. Data: `analysis/corridors/tmo_corridors.csv`, generated by
`tools/sample_corridors.py`.

## Why this was needed

Every corridor-level claim in the study rested on two routes. After the
site-selection artifact was removed, the corrected rural corridor showed only
a ~7 pp delivery penalty against urban and no coverage gap at all, which
raised an obvious question the two-route sample cannot answer: **is that
corridor typical?**

It also matters for the controller. The regime finding says admission control
helps when a link is contention-limited and does almost nothing when it is
coverage-limited. Both case-study corridors turned out to be coverage-limited,
so the controller's real-world population was unknown.

## Method, and what it does not model

300 corridors sampled from the national OpenCellID extract for one operator
(T-Mobile, mcc310-mnc260; 211,010 LTE cells, restricted to CONUS). Each
corridor is a 20 km straight drive on a random bearing, anchored on a real
cell so it is not sampling empty ocean. For each, the distance from 200 route
samples to the nearest cell is computed with a KD-tree.

**Tower positions are real; road topology is idealised.** The regime is set by
site spacing rather than road curvature, and the existing corridorA scenarios
already drive straight chains, so this is the right trade for this question --
but it should be stated plainly rather than glossed. OpenCellID positions are
also *estimated* cell locations from crowd-sourced measurements, and an absent
record is not evidence of absent service (see `docs/TOWER_DATA.md`).

## The distribution

Distance to the nearest cell along the drive, across 300 corridors:

| | p10 | p25 | p50 | p75 | p90 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| median | 334 m | 571 m | 1,042 m | 2,020 m | 3,713 m |
| p95 | 1,096 m | 1,875 m | 3,173 m | 5,267 m | 7,933 m |
| max | 1,321 m | 2,199 m | 3,581 m | 5,728 m | 8,790 m |

Fraction of the drive beyond the ~7 km usable horizon (−104 dBm):

| threshold | corridors | share | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| any part out of range | 52 / 300 | **17.3%** | [13.5, 22.0] |
| >5% of drive | 44 / 300 | 14.7% | [11.1, 19.1] |
| >10% of drive | 35 / 300 | **11.7%** | [8.5, 15.8] |
| ~~>25% of drive~~ | ~~5 / 300~~ | ~~1.7%~~ | **struck — measures the design's 0.30 ceiling, not coverage; see audit A5** |

All three surviving shares are **lower bounds** (audit A1, A4).

## Where the case studies sit — the main finding

| Corridor | median | p95 | max | national percentile (max) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DC urban case study | 110 m | 212 m | 249 m | **0.0th** |
| VA rural case study | 447 m | 2,041 m | 2,413 m | **27th** |

**The "rural" case-study corridor is better covered than 73% of sampled US
corridors.** It is not a representative rural route; it is a comparatively
well-served one. The urban case study is at the extreme of density — no
sampled corridor is better covered.

So the two case studies bracket a narrow and unrepresentative slice of the
national population: one extraordinarily dense, one above average.

## What follows

1. **The corrected rural result is optimistic.** Reporting 91.3% delivery on
   the VA corridor as "the rural case" understates the national picture, since
   73% of corridors are worse covered. This should be stated wherever that
   number appears.

2. **It partly restores the coverage-disparity argument, on firmer evidence.**
   Not "there is a measured gap on this route" -- that claim was the artifact
   -- but "17.3% of sampled corridors have stretches beyond usable range, and
   11.7% have more than a tenth of the drive out of range". That is a
   distributional claim about infrastructure, supported by a sample rather
   than by one hand-picked route.

3. **It bounds the controller's population.** Corridors that are
   coverage-limited are ones where admission control cannot help. At least
   11.7% fall there outright. How many of the remainder are contention-limited
   -- the regime where the controller pays -- still requires simulation of a
   stratified sample, which is the next step.

## Limitations

Single operator, single corridor length, straight-line routes, one horizon
threshold. The 7 km horizon comes from the −104 dBm setting used in the rural
scenarios and is itself a modelling choice. Sampling is anchored on cells, so
corridors in genuinely unserved areas are under-represented — the true share
of poorly covered driving is likely higher than 17.3%, not lower.

## Adversarial audit (2026-11-04)

This study had never had one. Every number above **reproduces exactly** from
`analysis/corridors/tmo_corridors.csv`, and the projection is correct —
`sample_corridors.py` builds a fresh azimuthal-equidistant projection centred
on each anchor (`Proj(proj='aeqd', lat_0=clat, lon_0=clon)`), which is the
right choice for point distances, so there is no lat/lon-as-Euclidean bug.
The cell-selection box (±0.45°, ≥33 km even in longitude at 49°N) is wide
enough that nearest-cell distances are exact out to ~23 km, far beyond any
observed value.

**No conclusion here is overturned.** Every bias found runs in the same
direction — it makes the sample look *better* covered than reality — so the
qualitative findings survive as conservative. But four things need restating
and two are wrong as written.

### A1. The corridor is CENTRED on the anchor cell, which censors both difficulty statistics

`route = t * (cos, sin)` for `t` in `linspace(-half, +half)`, in a projection
centred on the anchor. So every corridor passes **directly over a real tower at
its exact midpoint**, with a guaranteed zero-distance sample. Two hard ceilings
follow:

| Statistic | Structural ceiling | Why | At the ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| `max_m` | 10,000 m | no route point is further than the half-length from the anchor | **9 / 300 exactly 10000.0** |
| `frac_beyond_horizon` | 0.300 | with a cell at the midpoint, only (10−7)/10 of each half can exceed 7 km | **2 / 300 exactly 0.3000** |

The doc above says the anchoring exists "so it is not sampling empty ocean".
That is true and materially understates it. In a genuinely unserved area the
true distance to the nearest cell might be 40 km; this design reports 10 km,
because it placed a tower in the middle of the route. **The right tail of the
difficulty distribution is censored, not merely under-sampled.**

### A2. c14 — the hardest simulated corridor — sits at BOTH ceilings

The 15 corridors in `stratified.csv` are all drawn from the 300 (verified: 15
of 15 match on coordinates), spaced evenly in `max_m` rank. c14 has
`max_m = 10000.0` and `frac_beyond_horizon = 0.3000`: both exactly at the
structural maximum.

So **c14's difficulty is a lower bound, and the simulated sample does not reach
the hard end of the distribution at all.** Any sentence of the form "the
controller still helps at the worst end of the national distribution" is
unsupported — this study cannot say how bad that end is. c13 is near the
ceiling too (9340 m).

### A3. The percentile labels were wrong, and are now corrected

`CORRIDOR_REGIME.md` derives labels from `NN/14 × 100`. Measured against the
300-corridor sample, that formula drifts and overstates the extreme:

| | c08 | c09 | c10 | c11 | c12 | c13 | c14 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| formula | 57th | 64th | 71st | 79th | 86th | 93rd | **100th** |
| measured | 57.0 | 64.0 | 71.3 | 78.3 | 85.3 | 92.7 | **97.0** |

`PCTILE` in `tools/analyze_static_oracle.py` and
`tools/analyze_corridor_ablation.py` now carries the measured values. c14 is
the **97th** percentile of this sample, not the 100th — and "100th percentile"
reads as "worst case nationally", which is wrong twice over: wrong within the
sample, and the sample is itself censored and urban-weighted (A1, A4).

### A4. The sampled population is not "US corridors" — it is weighted by cell count

Anchors are drawn uniformly over **cells**, not over sites, road-km or area.
Dense metros carry many sectors and bands per site *and* many sites, so
selection probability is super-linear in density. The result:

- median sampled corridor has **1,239 sites within 50 km**
- **56% (167/300)** have more than 1,000 sites within 50 km
- quartiles of `n_sites_50km`: p10 124, p25 420, p50 1,239, p75 3,449, p90 5,805

That is not a sample of US driving. Wherever a percentile is quoted, the
population must be stated as *corridors drawn in proportion to one operator's
LTE cell density, each passing through a cell at its midpoint* — not "US
corridors". The limitations section above attributes the optimism to
cell-anchoring avoiding blank map, which is the weaker of the two mechanisms.

### A5. The ">25% of drive" row measures the ceiling, not coverage — struck

All 5 of those corridors lie within 0.05 of the 0.300 structural maximum, and 2
are exactly at it. **That row should be removed**, not re-interpreted.

### A6. The bolded shares need intervals

From n = 300, Wilson 95%:

| threshold | corridors | share | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| any part out of range | 52 / 300 | 17.3% | **[13.5, 22.0]** |
| >5% of drive | 44 / 300 | 14.7% | [11.1, 19.1] |
| >10% of drive | 35 / 300 | 11.7% | **[8.5, 15.8]** |
| ~~>25% of drive~~ | ~~5 / 300~~ | ~~1.7%~~ | struck, see A5 |

Quoting "17.3%" and "11.7%" to three significant figures from 300 samples
overstates the precision; both are lower bounds with ±4 pp of sampling error
on top.

### What survives, and is now better supported

The main finding is **robust to the choice of statistic**, which is worth
saying because `max_m` is the noisiest thing in the table:

| | by median | by max |
|---|---|---|
| DC urban case study | 0.3rd pct | 0.0th pct |
| VA rural case study | **19.0th pct** | **27.0th pct** |

So "the rural case study is better covered than 73% of sampled corridors" holds
either way, and under A1/A4 the true geographic figure would be **higher**, not
lower. The claim is conservative. Likewise 17.3% and 11.7% remain valid as
lower bounds on the share of poorly covered driving, which is what the original
limitations section already said.
